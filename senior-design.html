EE 4910 Senior Design

Biomimetic Prosthesis with Precise Recreation of Hand Movement

Iowa State University Senior Design

EMG • Signal Processing • Embedded Systems • Prosthetics

Project Overview

The goal of our senior design project is to develop a biomimetic prosthetic hand capable of recreating useful human hand movements. The system combines electromyography (EMG), embedded electronics, sensing, control, and mechanical actuation to investigate how biological muscle activity can be translated into movement of a prosthetic hand.

The project is being developed by a multidisciplinary electrical and computer engineering team. Major areas of development include EMG signal acquisition and processing, hand actuation, embedded control, electronics, and pressure and temperature sensing.

My Role – EMG & Signal Processing

My primary responsibility is research and development of the EMG and signal-processing portion of the prosthetic system. Surface electromyography (sEMG) provides a method of measuring electrical activity generated by muscles in the forearm. These signals can provide information that can ultimately be used to determine the intended movement of the prosthetic hand.

My work has included researching available EMG sensors, comparing commercial and custom sensing approaches, investigating sensor placement and the number of channels required, and determining how EMG signals can be acquired and processed by the electronics in the prosthetic system.

I have also been involved in establishing a testing environment for EMG sensors and investigating the feasibility of developing a custom EMG acquisition PCB to reduce cost and provide greater control over the final design.

EMG Signal Acquisition

EMG signals are low-amplitude analog signals generated by muscle activity. A major challenge for the project is reliably acquiring these signals from the forearm and converting them into data that can be used by the prosthetic control system.

Current research includes determining appropriate surface EMG sensors, evaluating the number of sensing channels required, identifying useful electrode locations, and understanding the hardware necessary to amplify, filter, sample, and process the signals.

Cost is also an important design constraint. Commercial multi-channel EMG systems can consume a significant portion of the project budget, so the team is investigating lower-cost sensors, available university hardware, and the possibility of developing custom EMG electronics.

System Architecture

The complete prosthetic system requires several subsystems to work together. The EMG subsystem acquires muscle activity from the user and provides information that can be converted into movement commands. Those commands must then interface with the embedded controller and the hand's actuation system.

Additional pressure and temperature sensors are being investigated to provide information about interactions between the prosthetic hand and its environment. This creates a system involving sensing, signal processing, embedded control, actuation, and user feedback.

Current Development

The project is currently in the research and early prototyping phase. The team has been defining the project scope, researching available prosthetic-hand platforms, evaluating sensing technologies, and determining the electronics and control architecture needed for implementation.

For the EMG subsystem, preliminary sensors are being acquired to gain hands-on experience with EMG signal acquisition and processing. The team is also investigating existing EMG hardware, available datasets, and custom PCB approaches before selecting the final architecture.

Engineering Challenges

  • Reliably acquiring low-amplitude EMG signals
  • Determining appropriate electrode placement
  • Selecting the number of EMG channels required
  • Filtering and processing biological signals
  • Interfacing EMG data with an embedded controller
  • Coordinating sensing information with hand actuation
  • Managing the number of required microcontroller I/O connections
  • Meeting project cost and hardware constraints
  • Integrating pressure and temperature feedback

Skills & Knowledge Gained

The project has expanded my understanding of biomedical signal acquisition and the challenges involved in applying electrical engineering to human-machine interfaces. My research has required me to learn about surface EMG sensing, analog signal acquisition, filtering, sampling, embedded-system interfaces, and sensor selection.

Senior design has also strengthened my ability to work on an open-ended engineering problem. Unlike a traditional laboratory assignment, the project does not begin with a predetermined solution. Our team must define requirements, compare possible technologies, work within a budget, communicate across different engineering areas, and make design decisions based on research and experimental results.

Big-Picture Contribution

My contribution focuses on creating the connection between the user and the prosthetic hand. The mechanical system can physically move the fingers, but the prosthesis also requires a reliable way to determine what movement the user intends to perform.

The EMG subsystem provides that interface by measuring muscle activity and converting biological signals into information that the electronic control system can use. My work on EMG sensing and signal processing therefore contributes to the larger goal of making the prosthetic hand respond naturally and intentionally to its user.
