Analog Integrated Circuit Design

PTAT Temperature Sensor

EE 3300 – Integrated Electronics

Cadence Virtuoso • CMOS • Analog IC Design • Layout • DRC • LVS

Project Overview

The goal of this project was to design and implement an analog temperature sensor with an output voltage proportional to absolute temperature (PTAT). The sensor was designed to operate from 0°C to 50°C and produce a linear output ranging from 0 V at 0°C to 1 V at 50°C.

The project included transistor-level circuit design, simulation, component sizing, physical IC layout, and design verification using Cadence Virtuoso.

Design Requirements

  • Temperature range of 0°C to 50°C
  • Output of 0 V at 0°C
  • Output of 1 V at 50°C
  • Linear PTAT output response
  • Use of diodes as temperature-sensitive devices
  • Implementation of an operational amplifier
  • Physical IC layout and design verification

My Role

I worked with a project partner to develop the complete temperature-sensor circuit. My work included circuit analysis, device sizing, simulation, operational-amplifier design, resistor-network tuning, physical layout, and troubleshooting DRC and LVS verification issues.

The project required us to move from an initial circuit concept through simulation and finally into a physical IC layout while maintaining the required temperature-to-voltage relationship.

Design & Implementation

Stage 1 – PTAT Generation

The first stage generated the temperature-dependent voltage. Two diodes with different device areas were biased using a current mirror. Maintaining equal current through the devices allowed the difference in diode voltage to depend primarily on their area ratio and temperature.

Different diode areas created different current densities, producing a voltage difference that varied proportionally with absolute temperature. A diode-area ratio of approximately 2.72 was selected for the design.

Stage 2 – Differential Amplification

The PTAT voltage generated by the first stage was very small, so a differential operational amplifier was designed to measure and amplify the difference between the two input voltages.

The operational amplifier was implemented at the transistor level using eight MOSFETs. Device dimensions were selected to maintain appropriate bias currents and transistor operating regions while providing sufficient gain and stability.

Output Scaling

A resistor network was used to provide both gain and output offset. The differential signal before amplification was only a few millivolts, requiring a large amplifier gain to produce the desired 0–1 V output range.

The final feedback network produced a gain of approximately -203.8. An additional voltage-divider network was used to establish the output offset, and a compensation capacitor was included to improve operational-amplifier stability.

Results

The completed circuit produced the desired linear temperature-to-voltage response over the 0°C to 50°C operating range.

Physical Layout & Verification

After verifying the schematic operation, the complete circuit was implemented as a physical layout. The layout included PMOS and NMOS devices, diodes, resistors, and the transistor-level differential amplifier. Metal 1, Metal 2, and polysilicon were used to route the circuit.

Design Rule Checking (DRC) was used to identify physical-layout violations. Layout Versus Schematic (LVS) verification was then used to compare the physical implementation against the original circuit schematic.

LVS debugging was one of the largest challenges of the project. Device-recognition and resistor-property errors required systematic troubleshooting and modifications to the layout. Resolving these issues provided valuable experience with integrated-circuit verification and debugging.

Skills & Knowledge Gained

This project strengthened my understanding of analog integrated circuit design, including current mirrors, diode behavior, differential amplifiers, MOSFET sizing, biasing, feedback, amplifier stability, and PTAT temperature sensing.

I also gained significant experience using Cadence Virtuoso for transistor-level simulation and physical IC layout. Debugging DRC and LVS errors helped develop my ability to troubleshoot complex designs and understand the relationship between a circuit schematic and its physical implementation.
